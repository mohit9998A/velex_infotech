"IT consulting" and "managed IT services" are sold by overlapping firms, described in near-identical language, and bought largely by accident — businesses tend to end up with whichever one the first vendor they called happened to sell. They solve different problems, and buying the wrong one is a recurring cost rather than a one-off mistake.

Here is the actual distinction and how to tell which you need.

## What is the difference between IT consulting and managed IT services?

IT consulting is advisory and finite: someone assesses a situation and recommends what to change. Managed IT services is operational and ongoing: someone runs your systems day to day for a recurring fee.

The clearest way to hold the distinction is by what you are buying. Consulting buys a decision. Managed services buys an outcome that has to keep being true — systems up, backups running, tickets answered. One ends with a document; the other only ends when you cancel.

The confusion is commercially useful to vendors, because a consulting engagement is a natural on-ramp to a managed contract, and many firms price the first accordingly. That is not necessarily against your interests, but it does mean the recommendation to move to managed services should be read with the same scepticism as any other vendor recommendation.

## When do you need IT consulting?

You need consulting when facing a decision with long consequences and no in-house expertise to evaluate it — a platform migration, a security posture review, a systems architecture that has stopped scaling, or a vendor proposal you cannot assess.

The common thread is that the decision is infrequent and expensive to get wrong. Nobody migrates their core systems annually, which means nobody accumulates the experience to do it well, which is precisely when buying that experience for a defined period makes sense.

Consulting is also the right purchase when you suspect you are already being badly served. An independent review of an existing IT arrangement — what you are paying for, what you are actually getting, what the exit looks like — is one of the higher-return engagements available, and it is rarely bought because the awkwardness of commissioning it falls on whoever chose the incumbent.

## When do you need managed IT services?

You need managed services when the work is continuous and operational: helpdesk, patching, backup verification, endpoint management and monitoring. If the requirement recurs every week, it is a managed-services requirement.

The sizing question is straightforward. Below roughly the point where a full-time internal hire is justified, outsourcing is usually cheaper and gives you coverage across more specialisms than one person could hold. Above it, an internal team plus targeted external support tends to win, because response time and institutional knowledge start to matter more than breadth.

What managed services does not do is decide anything strategic. A managed provider keeps the current arrangement working well; they are not the right party to ask whether the current arrangement should exist. That is the consulting question, and asking your managed provider to answer it puts them in an obvious conflict.

## Can one vendor do both?

Yes, and many do — but the advisory work should be separable and portable. If the recommendation only makes sense as an input to that vendor's managed contract, you received a proposal rather than an assessment.

The practical safeguard is to commission the assessment as its own paid engagement with its own deliverable, and to be explicit that you may implement it elsewhere. Vendors who are comfortable with that are usually the ones worth hiring for the implementation anyway. Vendors who resist have told you what the assessment was for.

There is a second safeguard worth applying: ask what the assessment would recommend if the answer were "keep doing what you are doing." A genuine review sometimes reaches that conclusion. A sales exercise never does.

## Where does AI fit into this?

AI consulting is a specialisation of the consulting side, not a separate category. It asks which business processes justify intelligence and which do not — and the same conflict-of-interest test applies.

The distinction that matters here is between IT and process. Traditional IT consulting concerns the systems that keep the business running: infrastructure, security, platforms. AI consulting concerns how work moves through the business and whether parts of it should be automated or delegated to an agent. The two overlap at the data layer, because an AI project fails on data readiness more often than on anything else, and data readiness is an IT question.

## How should you evaluate either type of vendor?

Evaluate consulting on portability of the deliverable and willingness to deliver bad news. Evaluate managed services on response-time commitments, what the exit looks like, and who owns your documentation.

For managed services specifically, three questions surface most of the risk:

- **What are the response-time commitments, and what happens when they are missed?** A commitment with no consequence is a marketing claim.
- **What does offboarding look like?** Documentation, credentials and configuration should transfer on request, not on negotiation.
- **Who holds the admin credentials to your systems?** If the answer is only the vendor, you have a dependency that is very expensive to unwind.

That third question is the one businesses most regret not asking. Credential control is the difference between changing vendors and rebuilding.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is managed IT cheaper than hiring internally?**
Below the threshold where a full-time hire is justified, usually yes — and you get broader coverage than one person provides. Above it, internal plus targeted external support typically wins.

**Can we start with consulting and move to managed services?**
Yes, and that is a common sequence. Just commission the assessment as its own engagement with a portable deliverable, so the recommendation is not shaped by the contract that might follow.

**Do we need both at the same time?**
Rarely. Consulting is episodic and managed services is continuous, so the usual pattern is a consulting engagement at a decision point and a managed contract running underneath it.

**How long should an IT consulting engagement last?**
Long enough to inspect the real environment and short enough to have a defined end. An advisory engagement with no end date has quietly become a managed contract without the service levels.

Consulting buys a decision and ends; managed services buys an ongoing outcome and does not. Buy consulting at infrequent, expensive decision points, buy managed services for work that recurs weekly, and keep the advisory work portable so the recommendation is not shaped by the contract that follows it.
